Airspace classes

The US divides the sky into controlled airspace (Classes A, B, C, D and E) and uncontrolled airspace (Class G). Each class has its own shape, entry rules and equipment requirements, and the written test expects you to know them all.

Class B: you need a clearance

Regardless of the weather, you need an actual ATC clearance to enter Class B. Listen for the words “cleared into the Class Bravo airspace.” Being in radio contact isn't enough. Inside, VFR pilots get sequencing and separation from ATC, and the VFR minimums are 3 SM and clear of clouds.

Class C and D: what “two-way radio communication” means

For Class C and D you don't need a clearance, but you must establish two-way radio communication before you cross the boundary. It's established when the controller answers using your call sign, even if all they say is “[your call sign], standby.” If they answer without your call sign (“aircraft calling, standby”), it isn't established and you must stay out. If they tell you to remain outside, you remain outside.

Many Class D towers aren't open all night. When the tower closes, the airspace usually becomes Class E or Class G, and the FAA Chart Supplement says which.

Class E and G: reading the vignettes

A magenta vignette (a fuzzy magenta band) means Class E begins at 700 ft AGL inside it. A blue vignette marks Class E beginning at 1,200 ft AGL. Below that floor is Class G. A dashed magenta line around an airport is a Class E surface area, which starts at the ground. Where no lower floor is designated, Class E starts at 14,500 ft MSL.

Transponder and ADS-B Out

Unless ATC authorizes otherwise, you need an altitude-reporting (Mode C) transponder and ADS-B Out:

  • In Class A, B and C airspace.
  • Within 30 NM of a Class B primary airport (the Mode C veil), from the surface to 10,000 ft MSL.
  • Above the ceiling and within the lateral limits of Class B or Class C airspace, up to 10,000 ft MSL.
  • At and above 10,000 ft MSL over the 48 contiguous states and DC, except at and below 2,500 ft AGL.

Speed limits

  • 250 knots indicated below 10,000 ft MSL.
  • 200 knots at or below 2,500 ft AGL within 4 NM of the primary airport of a Class C or Class D area.
  • 200 knots under a Class B shelf or in a VFR corridor through Class B.

    A. Class A

  2. What is required to enter Class B airspace?

    1. AAn ATC clearance, two-way radio, a Mode C transponder, and ADS-B Out
    2. BTwo-way radio contact only
    3. CNothing, if you stay VFR
    Show answer

    A. An ATC clearance, two-way radio, a Mode C transponder, and ADS-B Out

    ATC must actually say 'cleared into the Class Bravo.' You also need a private pilot certificate, or a student pilot certificate with the specific endorsement.

    14 CFR 91.131 · Open in Zarhart Prep

  3. What is required to enter Class C airspace?

    1. AAn explicit ATC clearance
    2. BTwo-way radio communication established with ATC before entering
    3. COnly a Mode C transponder
    Show answer

    B. Two-way radio communication established with ATC before entering

    If the controller answers using your call sign, communication is established. 'Aircraft calling, standby' does not count. Typical size: 5 NM core from the surface to 4,000 AGL, and a 10 NM shelf from 1,200 to 4,000 AGL.

    14 CFR 91.130 · Open in Zarhart Prep

  4. A Class D surface area on a sectional shows [29] in a dashed blue box. To fly over it without entering, you must be

    1. Aabove 2,900 ft AGL
    2. Babove 2,900 ft MSL
    3. Cabove 2,500 ft MSL
    Show answer

    B. above 2,900 ft MSL

    Bracketed numbers are the Class D ceiling in hundreds of feet MSL. A minus sign before the number means 'up to but not including' that altitude.

    Aeronautical Chart User's Guide · Open in Zarhart Prep

  5. How far does the Mode C veil extend from a Class B primary airport?

    2. B10 NM, surface to 4,000 ft AGL
    3. C30 NM, from the surface to 10,000 ft MSL

    C. 30 NM, from the surface to 10,000 ft MSL

    Inside it you need an altitude-reporting transponder and ADS-B Out.
