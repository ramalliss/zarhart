VASI and PAPI

Visual glide slope indicators use red and white lights beside the runway to show whether you're high, low or on a safe approach path. They give vertical guidance only, and in Class B, C and D airspace a regulation says you must stay at or above them.

Zarhart study notes · Sources listed below · Updated October 2026

2-bar VASI

Two bars of lights beside the runway: a near bar and a far bar. Each light looks white from above its beam and red from below. The glide path is normally 3°.

You seeYou are
Red over whiteOn the glide path
White over whiteToo high
Red over redToo low
Memory aidRed over white, you're all right. White over white, too high. Red over red, too low.

3-bar VASI

A middle bar gives two glide paths. The near and middle bars give the normal path, usually 3°. The middle and far bars give a path about ¼° higher, meant only for airplanes with high cockpits, like large jets. In a trainer, use the near and middle bars exactly like a 2-bar VASI.

PAPI

A single row of two or four lights, usually on the left side of the runway. With four lights:

You seeYou are
4 whiteAbove the glide path
3 white, 1 redSlightly high
2 white, 2 redOn the glide path
1 white, 3 redSlightly low
4 redBelow the glide path

What they guarantee, and what they don't

  • Obstruction clearance within 10° either side of the extended runway centerline, out to 4 NM from the threshold for a VASI and 3.4 NM for a PAPI.
  • No course guidance. The runway and its lights give you that. Don't start down the VASI or PAPI until you're lined up with the runway.
  • Range. A VASI is visible about 3 to 5 miles by day and 20 miles or more at night; a PAPI about 5 miles by day and up to 20 at night.
  • The rule. An airplane approaching to land on a runway served by a visual approach slope indicator must stay at or above the glide path until a lower altitude is necessary for a safe landing. This is 14 CFR 91.129(e)(3), for Class D airspace, and Class C and B operations must also comply with 91.129.

You may also meet tri-color indicators (amber above, green on path, red below), pulsating indicators (pulsing white above, steady white or alternating red and white on path, steady red slightly below, pulsing red below) and unlit alignment-of-elements panels at small airports.

  1. On final approach to a runway with a standard 2-bar VASI, red over white means you are

    1. Aon the glidepath
    2. Babove the glidepath
    3. Cbelow the glidepath
    Show answer

    A. on the glidepath

    Red over white, you're all right. White over white, you're too high. Red over red, you're too low.

    AIM 2-1-2 · Open in Zarhart Prep

  2. Which approach and landing objective is assured when the pilot remains on the proper glidepath of the VASI?

    1. ACourse guidance from the visual descent point to touchdown
    2. BSafe obstruction clearance in the approach area
    3. CContinuation of course guidance after transition to VFR
    Show answer

    B. Safe obstruction clearance in the approach area

    A VASI gives vertical guidance only, with no course guidance. It guarantees obstruction clearance within ±10° of the extended centerline, out to 4 NM.

  3. While operating in Class D airspace, each pilot of an aircraft approaching to land on a runway served by a VASI shall

    1. Amaintain an altitude at or below the glide slope
    2. Bstay on the glide slope until 50 ft above the threshold
    3. Cmaintain an altitude at or above the glide slope until a lower altitude is necessary for a safe landing
    Show answer

    C. maintain an altitude at or above the glide slope until a lower altitude is necessary for a safe landing

    This is a regulation, not just advice.
