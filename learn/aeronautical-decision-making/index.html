Aeronautical decision-making

Most accidents start with a decision, not a lack of stick-and-rudder skill. The FAA's decision-making tools are short checklists that help you spot a bad decision while there's still time to change it.

Zarhart study notes · Sources listed below · Updated October 2026

The five hazardous attitudes

AttitudeSounds likeAntidote
Anti-authority“Don't tell me.”“Follow the rules. They are usually right.”
Impulsivity“Do it quickly.”“Not so fast. Think first.”
Invulnerability“It won't happen to me.”“It could happen to me.”
Macho“I can do it.”“Taking chances is foolish.”
Resignation“What's the use?”“I'm not helpless. I can make a difference.”

The test gives you a pilot's thought or action and asks which attitude it shows, or which antidote fits. Learn the pairs.

Check yourself and the flight

  • IMSAFE checks the pilot: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion (some versions use Eating).
  • PAVE sorts the risks of a flight: Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment (weather, terrain, airports, airspace, night) and External pressures (schedules, passengers, get-there-itis).

Make the decision

  • DECIDE: Detect that something changed, Estimate the need to react, Choose an outcome, Identify actions, Do the best action, Evaluate the result.
  • 3P: Perceive the hazards, Process their risk, Perform risk management. Repeat it on the ground and throughout the flight.

Assessing risk

A risk matrix combines likelihood (improbable, remote, occasional, probable) with severity (negligible, marginal, critical, catastrophic). High likelihood and catastrophic severity means don't go, or change the plan until the risk drops. Personal minimums (your own ceiling, visibility, wind and fuel limits, set above the legal ones) make the call before the pressure starts.

Operational pitfalls

  • Get-there-itis (plan continuation bias): pressing on to the destination as conditions get worse.
  • Scud running: descending under lowering clouds to stay VFR, toward terrain and towers.
  • Continuing VFR into instrument conditions: spatial disorientation and loss of control often follow within minutes.
  • Getting behind the airplane and loss of situational awareness.
  • Peer pressure and flying with too little fuel reserve.

Use every resource

Single-pilot resource management means using what you have: ATC flight following and Flight Service, the passengers (a second set of eyes, a radio-frequency holder), the autopilot and GPS, and a plan B airport picked before you need it.
