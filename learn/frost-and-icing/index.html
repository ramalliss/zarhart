Frost and structural icing

Ice on an airplane changes the shape and roughness of the wing, adds weight and drag, and raises the stall speed. Light training airplanes aren't approved to fly in known icing, so the knowledge test focuses on recognising the conditions and staying out of them.

Zarhart study notes · Sources listed below · Updated October 2026

Frost

Frost forms when the surface of the airplane is at or below the dew point of the surrounding air, and that dew point is below freezing: water vapour turns straight into ice crystals, typically on a clear, calm night.

Frost looks harmless, but it roughens the top of the wing enough to spoil the smooth airflow. Lift goes down, drag goes up, and the wing stalls at a lower angle of attack, which means a higher speed. The airplane may not fly at its normal takeoff speed. Remove all of it before flight: brush or wipe it off, use deicing fluid, or warm the airplane in a hangar. Don't count on it blowing off during the takeoff roll.

Structural icing needs two things

The two ingredientsVisible moisture (cloud, rain or drizzle) and an airframe temperature at or below freezing. Take away either one and structural ice can't form.

TypeHow it formsWhat it's like
RimeSmall supercooled droplets freeze the instant they hit, trapping air.Rough, milky and brittle. Common in stratus clouds.
ClearLarge supercooled drops, such as freezing rain, spread back over the surface before they freeze.Hard, heavy, glossy and hard to remove. The most dangerous kind: it builds fast.
MixedA mix of drop sizes, often with snow or ice particles.Rough and lumpy, with the hazards of both.

Freezing rain and ice pellets

Rain that freezes on contact means it formed in warmer, above-freezing air higher up and then fell through a freezing layer near the ground: a temperature inversion. Ice pellets (sleet) at the surface tell you the same thing: there is freezing rain at a higher altitude. Freezing rain is the fastest way to collect clear ice.

What ice does to the airplane

EffectWhy it matters
Lift decreasesThe wing's shape and surface change, so it stalls at a lower angle of attack and a higher speed.
Drag increasesYou need more power to hold altitude and speed.
Weight increasesHeavier and less lift: the airplane may not be able to stay level.
Thrust decreasesIce on the propeller makes it less efficient.
Instruments failAn iced-over pitot tube makes the airspeed indicator drop to zero if its drain hole stays open. Turn on pitot heat before you enter visible moisture near freezing.

If you pick up ice

Get out of the conditions right away: reverse course to where you came from, or change altitude to get out of the cloud or into above-freezing air, and tell ATC. Land as soon as practical with extra speed, because the stall speed is higher, and avoid steep turns and full flaps if the tail may be iced. Carburetor ice is a separate problem: it can form in clear, humid air well above freezing.

  1. On a cold morning you find a thin layer of frost on the wings. What should you do?

    1. ATake off: the frost will blow off during the takeoff roll
    2. BRotate at a higher speed to make up for it
    3. CRemove all of it before takeoff
    Show answer

    C. Remove all of it before takeoff

    Even thin frost roughens the wing, decreasing lift and increasing drag, and the airplane may not fly at the normal takeoff speed. It cannot be relied on to blow off.

  2. Frost forms on an aircraft when

    1. Asmall drops of rain freeze on contact
    2. Bthe collecting surface is at or below the dew point of the adjacent air, and the dew point is below freezing
    3. Cthe air temperature is below freezing and there is fog
    Show answer

    B. the collecting surface is at or below the dew point of the adjacent air, and the dew point is below freezing

    Frost is water vapor depositing directly as ice. Remove all of it before flight: it disrupts airflow, reduces lift and raises stall speed.

  3. For structural icing to form, there must be

    1. Ahigh humidity and temperatures between 20°F and 70°F
    2. Bvisible moisture and an airframe temperature at or below freezing
    3. Cclear skies and below-freezing temperatures
    Show answer

    B. visible moisture and an airframe temperature at or below freezing

    Structural ice needs visible moisture (cloud, rain, drizzle) and a surface at or below 0°C.

    PHAK ch. 12 · Open in Zarhart Prep

  4. Clear ice is most likely to form from

    1. Asmall supercooled droplets that freeze instantly
    2. Blarge supercooled water drops, such as freezing rain, that spread out before they freeze
    3. Cwater vapour turning directly into ice on a cold surface
    Show answer

    B. large supercooled water drops, such as freezing rain, that spread out before they freeze

    Large drops flow back over the surface before freezing, forming hard, heavy, glossy clear ice. Small droplets freeze on impact as rime; vapour deposition forms frost.
