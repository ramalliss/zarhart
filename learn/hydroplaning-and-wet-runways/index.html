Hydroplaning and wet runways

Water between the tires and the runway takes away the friction that brakes and steering depend on. Above a certain speed the tires can ride up on the water entirely. The knowledge test asks about the three kinds of hydroplaning, the speed rule of thumb and how to land on a wet runway.

Zarhart study notes · Sources listed below · Updated October 2026

The three kinds of hydroplaning

TypeWhat happensWhen to expect it
DynamicStanding water builds up in front of the tire and lifts it off the pavement. The tire rides on the water like a water ski.Standing water deeper than the tire tread, at high groundspeed.
ViscousA very thin film of water acts as a lubricant on a smooth surface, so the tire can't grip.Smooth runways and rubber deposits in the touchdown zone. It can happen at lower speeds than dynamic hydroplaning.
Reverted rubberA locked wheel skids, heat from the friction turns the water to steam, and the tire rides on the steam.After a prolonged locked-wheel skid. Avoid it by never locking the brakes on a wet runway.

The speed rule of thumb

The minimum speed for dynamic hydroplaning is about 9 times the square root of the tire pressure in pounds per square inch.

Worked examples36 psi: 9 × 6 = 54 knots. 25 psi: 9 × 5 = 45 knots. A Cessna 172's main tires at about 29 psi: 9 × 5.4 ≈ 48 knots.

Above that groundspeed on standing water, the brakes and the nosewheel steering do almost nothing. The tires come back down only as the airplane slows. Once a tire starts hydroplaning, it can keep doing so to somewhat lower speeds.

Landing on a wet or flooded runway

DoWhy
Fly a stabilized approach at the normal speed and land on the centrelineExtra speed means a longer float and more time above hydroplaning speed.
Touch down firmly, not with a long, gentle floatA positive touchdown helps the tires break through the water film to the pavement.
Hold the nose up for aerodynamic brakingThe wing's drag slows the airplane without needing tire friction.
Brake gently, and only once you're slowerHard braking while hydroplaning does nothing, and a locked wheel can start reverted rubber hydroplaning.
Use crosswind controls the whole wayWith little side grip, a crosswind can push the airplane toward the edge of the runway.
Expect a longer landing roll, and add it to your planningWet pavement cuts braking. Standing water, slush and snow cut it further.

Runway condition reports help you plan: ATIS and NOTAMs give braking action as good, medium or poor (and nil for ice), and on many runways a runway condition code from 6 (dry) down to 0.

Fly it

Land in heavy rain on a flooded runway

The Rain lesson puts you on final to Zarhart Field in heavy rain with standing water on runway 27. Brake too early and the tires hydroplane; wait until below about 48 knots and they grip.

  1. What are the three basic types of hydroplaning?

    1. ADynamic, static, and kinetic
    2. BViscous, frictional, and thermal
    3. CDynamic, reverted rubber, and viscous
    Show answer

    C. Dynamic, reverted rubber, and viscous

    Dynamic: standing water lifts the tire off the runway. Viscous: a thin film of water on a smooth surface. Reverted rubber: a locked, skidding wheel heats the rubber and turns the water to steam.

    2. B54 knots
    3. C72 knots

    B. 54 knots

    Hydroplaning speed ≈ 9 × √(tire pressure in psi) = 9 × 6 = 54 knots.

  3. Landing on a runway with standing water, the best way to slow down at high speed is to

    1. Aapply maximum braking right after touchdown
    2. Btouch down fast to get through the water quickly
    3. Chold the nose up for aerodynamic braking, and brake gently only once below hydroplaning speed
    Show answer

    C. hold the nose up for aerodynamic braking, and brake gently only once below hydroplaning speed

    Above the hydroplaning speed the tires ride on the water and the brakes do almost nothing. Land at the slowest safe speed, use aerodynamic braking, then brake gently.

    It follows a prolonged locked-wheel skid. Avoid it by not locking the brakes on a wet runway.
