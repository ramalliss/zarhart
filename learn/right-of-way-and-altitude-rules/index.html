Right-of-way and altitude rules

Four short regulations keep airplanes apart from each other and from the ground. They come up again and again on the written test, usually as “which aircraft has the right-of-way” or “what altitude should you fly.”

Zarhart study notes · Sources listed below · Updated October 2026

Right-of-way (14 CFR 91.113)

Whatever the rule, every pilot must keep looking and avoid collisions. When the rules say you have the right-of-way, you still give way if you need to.

SituationWho has the right-of-way
An aircraft in distressOver all other traffic
Converging, different categoriesThe less maneuverable: balloon, then glider, then airship, then airplane or rotorcraft. An aircraft towing or refueling another has it over other engine-driven aircraft
Converging, same categoryThe aircraft on the right
Head-onNeither: both alter course to the right
OvertakingThe aircraft being overtaken. Pass well clear on its right
LandingThe aircraft at the lower altitude, which may not use that to cut in front of another on final

Minimum safe altitudes (91.119)

  • Anywhere: high enough that if the engine fails you can make an emergency landing without undue hazard to people or property below.
  • Over congested areas (cities, towns, settlements, open-air assemblies): 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 2,000 ft horizontally.
  • Elsewhere: 500 ft above the surface. Over open water or sparsely populated areas, no closer than 500 ft to any person, vessel, vehicle or structure.
  • National parks and wildlife refuges ask for at least 2,000 ft above the surface (AIM 7-5-6). That's a request, not a regulation.

VFR cruising altitudes (91.159)

Magnetic courseFly atExamples
0° to 179°Odd thousands + 500 ft MSL3,500, 5,500, 7,500
180° to 359°Even thousands + 500 ft MSL4,500, 6,500, 8,500

The rule applies in level cruise more than 3,000 ft above the ground, and it's based on magnetic course, not heading. A memory aid: east is odd, west is even (plus 500).

Speed limits (91.117)

WhereMaximum indicated airspeed
Below 10,000 ft MSL250 knots
At or below 2,500 ft AGL within 4 NM of the primary airport of Class C or D airspace200 knots
Under the lateral limits of Class B airspace, or in a VFR corridor through it200 knots

Aerobatics and parachutes

  • Aerobatics (91.303): not over a congested area or an open-air assembly, not within Class B, C, D or E surface areas, not within 4 NM of a federal airway's centerline, not below 1,500 ft AGL and not with less than 3 SM flight visibility.
  • Parachutes (91.307): with a passenger aboard, each occupant must wear an approved parachute for any intentional bank over 60° or pitch over 30° up or down, except during flight training for a certificate.

    Show answer

    A. Alter course to the right

    In a head-on approach, each pilot alters course to the right.

  2. An airplane is overtaking another airplane. Which way should the overtaking pilot pass?

    1. ATo the right
    2. BTo the left
    3. CAbove
    Show answer

    A. To the right

    The aircraft being overtaken has the right-of-way; the overtaking aircraft passes well clear on the right.

    14 CFR 91.113(f) · Open in Zarhart Prep

  3. VFR cruising more than 3,000 ft AGL on a magnetic course of 090°, which altitude is appropriate?

    1. AEven thousands plus 500 ft, such as 6,500
    2. BOdd thousands, such as 5,000
    3. COdd thousands plus 500 ft, such as 5,500
    Show answer

    C. Odd thousands plus 500 ft, such as 5,500

    Magnetic courses 0° to 179°: odd thousands plus 500 ft. 180° to 359°: even thousands plus 500 ft.
