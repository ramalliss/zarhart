Microbursts and wind shear

Wind shear is a sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. It can happen at any altitude, but it's most dangerous close to the ground, where there's no height to give away. The most violent kind is a microburst under a thunderstorm or rain shaft.

Zarhart study notes · Sources listed below · Updated October 2026

Where wind shear comes from

  • Thunderstorms and convective showers, including microbursts and gust fronts.
  • Fronts, especially fast-moving cold fronts.
  • Temperature inversions: a strong wind above a calm, cold layer near the ground, often at night or early morning.
  • Strong surface winds blowing over buildings, trees and hills, plus mountain waves.

The microburst, in numbers

FeatureTypical value (AIM 7-1-24)
DowndraftLess than 1 mile across as it descends. It can be as strong as 6,000 feet per minute.
Outflow near the groundSpreads out to about 2½ miles across, with horizontal winds up to 45 knots: a 90-knot headwind-to-tailwind change for an airplane flying through.
Height of the strongest windsWithin a few hundred feet of the ground.
LifespanSeldom more than 15 minutes from first contact with the ground. The strongest winds last about 2 to 4 minutes, but more microbursts in the same area are likely.
Visual cluesHeavy rain under a storm, or light rain and virga that look benign. With little rain, a ring of blowing dust may be the only sign.

Flying through one on approach

Where you areWhat the wind doesWhat you see
Entering the outflowAn increasing headwindAirspeed jumps and the airplane balloons above the glide path. This is the warning.
The coreA strong downdraftThe airplane sinks hard even with power.
Leaving the outflowA tailwindAirspeed falls away just as you're low and slow: the dangerous part.

The trapThe first sign, a gain in airspeed and lift, tempts you to reduce power. Then the downdraft and tailwind arrive together. If the airspeed jumps unexpectedly on final near a storm, go around at once: full power, pitch up to stop the descent, and don't lower the nose close to the ground to regain airspeed.

How to avoid it

Don't take off or land with a thunderstorm or heavy rain shaft near the airport. Listen for wind shear and microburst alerts on the ATIS and from the tower, and pass on what you meet in a PIREP. Avoid any thunderstorm identified as severe or giving an intense radar echo by at least 20 miles. Waiting 15 to 20 minutes, or diverting, costs far less than flying through one.

  1. A microburst's downdraft can be as strong as

    1. A6,000 feet per minute
    2. B1,500 feet per minute
    3. C500 feet per minute
    Show answer

    A. 6,000 feet per minute

    Microbursts are small (about 1 km or less), short-lived (2 to 4 minutes at peak) downdrafts up to 6,000 fpm, with horizontal winds up to 45 knots.

    AIM 7-1-24 · Open in Zarhart Prep

  2. Flying into a microburst on approach, you would first expect

    1. Aan immediate strong tailwind
    2. Ba downdraft before any change in airspeed
    3. Can increasing headwind and a performance increase
    Show answer

    C. an increasing headwind and a performance increase

    First a headwind (airspeed up, the airplane balloons), then the downdraft, then a tailwind that can leave you low and slow.

    AIM 7-1-24 · Open in Zarhart Prep

  3. Flying through a microburst on approach, after the initial headwind you would encounter

    1. Aa steady headwind all the way to the runway
    2. Ban updraft that improves climb performance
    3. Ca downdraft and then a tailwind, decreasing performance
    Show answer

    C. a downdraft and then a tailwind, decreasing performance

    First an increasing headwind (performance increase), then the core downdraft, then a tailwind that drops the airspeed: often too much for a light airplane low on approach.

    AIM 7-1-24 · Open in Zarhart Prep

  4. An individual microburst usually lasts about

    1. A15 minutes from when it first strikes the ground
    2. B2 to 3 hours
    3. C30 seconds
    Show answer

    A. 15 minutes from when it first strikes the ground

    A microburst seldom lasts longer than 15 minutes from the time it strikes the ground until it dissipates, so waiting it out or diverting is the answer.
